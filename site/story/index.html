Heat · Health · Surveillance

Heat kills far more people than the records show

From the Sahel to the World Cup to American military bases, heat illness is mostly invisible in the systems built to count it. Prevention is taking hold where someone controls the schedule.

By Jarah Jacquay · October 2026 · Independent analysis. Quotations come from the published reports and news coverage listed under Sources.

    United States, Canada, Mexico · June 2026

    The game began stopping itself

    Midway through each half of every match at the 2026 World Cup, referees halted play for three minutes so players could drink and cool down. FIFA made the breaks mandatory “regardless of temperatures,” according to the Associated Press, even in enclosed or air-conditioned stadiums. Douglas Casa of the Korey Stringer Institute said his group was suggesting breaks of “something like five or six minutes,” the AP reported.

    Lost in the argument was how unusual the decision was. A governing body that sets the schedule had built rest into it before anyone collapsed.

    Worldwide

    A toll counted mostly by models

    The 2025 Lancet Countdown on health and climate change estimated that heat-related deaths averaged 546,000 a year in 2012–21, and that the heat-related death rate had risen 23 percent since the 1990s. Heat exposure cost an estimated 640 billion potential working hours in 2024.

    Almost none of those deaths appear in a register under the word “heat.”

    The pattern

    The certified count is the floor

    Wherever both kinds of number exist, the recorded count is far smaller than the estimate. In the United States in 2023, 2,325 death certificates listed heat as a cause, according to provisional data analyzed in JAMA; a researcher applying a published excess-death method told the AP the true toll was likely about 11,000. Spain attributed 3,832 deaths to heat in summer 2025 and confirmed 25 as heat stroke. From 2018 to 2022, fewer than half of the U.S. military's diagnosed heat-stroke encounters had a matching report in its reportable-illness system; by 2025, 77.4 percent of heat-stroke cases were reported.

    In India's 2024 hot season, provisional health ministry figures reported by the Press Trust of India counted at least 110 deaths and about 40,000 suspected heatstroke cases between March 1 and June 18.

    Bamako, Mali · April 2024

    Four days in the Sahel

    Gabriel Touré Hospital recorded 102 deaths from April 1 to 4, 2024, local media reported, as temperatures in the western city of Kayes reached 48.5 °C. In all of April 2023, the hospital had recorded 130. World Weather Attribution concluded that heat of that magnitude “would have been impossible” without the warming already observed. The causes of the hospital deaths were not reported.

    In Karachi that June, the Edhi Foundation said it received 568 bodies in five days. The local government said 13 people had died of weather-related causes in the city that month.

    Qatar · 2009–2017

    Recorded as cardiac arrest

    A 2019 study in Cardiology examined deaths among Nepali migrant workers in Qatar. Cardiovascular causes accounted for 22 percent of deaths in the cool season and 58 percent in the hot season. The authors estimated that as many as 200 of 571 cardiovascular deaths might have been prevented with effective heat protection.

    Mecca · June 2024

    Outside the system

    Saudi authorities reported 1,301 deaths during the 2024 Hajj, amid high temperatures at the holy sites. Officials said 83 percent of the dead were not authorized to perform it and had walked long distances in direct sunlight without adequate shelter: people outside the permit system that organizes transport and shade for registered pilgrims.

    32 European countries

    Europe counts carefully, and the numbers keep coming

    A study in Nature Medicine covering 654 regions estimated 67,873 heat-related deaths in Europe in summer 2022, 50,798 in 2023 and 62,775 in 2024. A separate rapid study of 854 cities, not peer reviewed and not comparable with that series, estimated about 24,400 heat deaths in summer 2025, about 16,500 of them attributable to climate change.

    Santé publique France attributed more than 5,700 deaths to heat in summer 2025, and nine fatal workplace accidents were notified as possibly heat-related. Spain's daily mortality monitoring attributed 3,832, up 87.6 percent from 2024.

    Maricopa County, Arizona · 2024

    Who dies

    The county Department of Public Health identified 608 heat-related deaths in 2024. Nearly half of those who died, 49 percent, were experiencing homelessness, and 77 percent of the deaths occurred outdoors. The people most exposed are those with the least control over where they are when the heat arrives.

    U.S. military · 2021–2025

    Twenty-one locations, at home and abroad

    Commanders decide when recruits run, march and drill. If any institution can prevent heat illness by controlling the schedule, it is a military training command. Yet 21 locations recorded at least 100 heat illness cases from 2021 through 2025, led by Fort Benning with 1,737. Okinawa recorded 409, Fort Shafter in Hawaii 177, and Naval Air Station Pensacola 137.

    In 2025 the active component recorded 518 heat strokes, and the heat-stroke rate rose for the second year in a row. Recruits had 3.5 times the heat-stroke rate of other enlisted members.

    By service · 2021–2025

    Not a single-service problem

    The Army carries most of the cases. The Navy's total rose every year in the five-year window, from 172 to 272. The Marine Corps peaked in 2024.

    Department of Defense report to Congress · 2023

    Below the warning

    The military's heat flags tell leaders when to cut back or stop. The Department's 2023 report to Congress found that from 1996 to 2019, 84 percent of heat illness among service members stationed in the continental United States occurred under conditions other than the black flag, and 20 percent on days with no flag at all, based on conditions reconstructed from nearby weather stations.

    It also stated that “there is no standardized tracking or archiving of WBGT readings or heat stress flag conditions at U.S. military training locations.”

    Alaska and beyond · 2020–2025

    The same logic, in the cold

    Active-duty cold weather injury rates rose 41.8 percent in the 2024–25 season, to 54.7 per 100,000 person-years. Over the five seasons through June 2025, Fort Wainwright and Joint Base Elmendorf-Richardson in Alaska recorded the most cases, in a region where, according to Defense Department coverage of its 2024 Arctic strategy, the services are to train more.

    What the numbers show

    Preventable where someone owns the conditions

    Not every measure is rising: the active-duty heat exhaustion rate fell 9.1 percent in 2025 even as heat stroke climbed. Part of Europe's modeled growth reflects aging populations and better methods.

    But the pattern holds. A football federation can change kickoff times. A pilgrimage authority can extend shade and medical coverage. A training command can move a run to dawn. What each needs first is a record of what the heat was when someone fell, kept long enough to learn from it.

    About the numbers

    Recorded counts come from death certificates, medical examiners, public health departments, hospital reports and reportable-illness systems. Estimates come from statistical models of excess or attributable mortality. The two answer different questions, and modeled and surveillance figures for the same country and year can differ by a factor of two or more. Compare within a chart row, never across rows. World Cup figures are pre-tournament projections, not measurements. Military counts are diagnoses among active-component service members reported in the Medical Surveillance Monthly Report; locations are the report's own groupings, and Okinawa is a single combined line. Military location counts have no population denominator and say nothing about individual risk.

    Corrections in this edition: the cold chart shows five-season totals (July 2020–June 2025), not the 2024–25 season; the Lancet death figure is a 2012–21 average; the Maricopa count is from the Department of Public Health; Karachi's figure came from the local government; India's 2024 deaths are provisional, not “confirmed”; the 2025 European rapid study is shown as separate from the 654-region series; the military's DRSi reporting share is dated (2018–22) with the 2025 figure added.

    Jarah Jacquay is a registered nurse and environmental health writer based in Pensacola, Florida. He has been admitted to the Doctor of Public Health program in environmental health at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health.
